# Angelfall: story kin, themes, and tutorial framing

Date: 2026-11-21 (UTC). Requested by: games-researcher.

## Questions

1. Which 6–10 novels, games, films or comics share Simple War's themes: reality/magic as an operating system (the Law) fraying and dying; an existential war where humanity is a latecomer catalyst; angels as counterfactual messengers of a broken history; staying human while fighting for existence?
2. How do tutorials and prologues introduce a world like Angelfall's — frontier evacuation under supernatural attack, escorting unarmed civilians to hangars, a conspiracy theorist who sees the pattern nobody believes?
3. How do time-linear campaigns (StarCraft / Brood War style) handle faction arcs and recurring NPCs, and what structures fit three human splinter factions plus a raid-boss-tier ally faction?
4. Pitfalls: shallow stay-human themes, lore dumps, chosen-one clichés, tonal whiplash — how did the best works keep depth?

## Short answer (5 lines)

- Closest kin: **Evangelion** (counterfactual angels), **The Laundry Files** and **A Fire Upon the Deep** (reality-as-system, fraying), **Mass Effect** and **Halo** (inherited infrastructure, humanity as latecomer catalyst), **StarCraft**, **Frostpunk** and **Spec Ops: The Line** (staying human under existential pressure).
- Best tutorial model: StarCraft II's Mar Sara arc — small local stakes teach the controls, then planetary evacuation recontextualises everything; Halo: Reach's *New Alexandria* is the direct mechanical ancestor of hangar escort.
- Jeff works as a Murray Bauman (Stranger Things): wrong about the frame, right about the pattern; recurring, and gated on the relationship.
- Structure: fixed chronological spine, one splinter per campaign arc, optional mission order (Wings of Liberty), Brood War-style interludes where factions intersect; Lawbringers enter late as raid-boss allies.
- Depth comes from irreversible systemic choices and retrospective judgement (Frostpunk), optional layered lore (Dark Souls), and never anointing the player chosen one (Dune Messiah).

## Findings

### Q1 — Story kin (8 works)

1. **Neon Genesis Evangelion** (anime, 1995). The Angels are an alternate form of humanity — 99.89% genetic match — descended from Adam, while humans descend from Lilith; humanity is itself the eighteenth Angel, an accidental usurper of a world the Angels were seeded to inherit. "Angel" is deliberately glossed as *messenger*. Each Angel is unique, attacks alone, and learns from prior battles; their names and attacks were prophesied in advance. The endgame offers the merger of all souls into one being — the stay-human question is whether painful individual existence is worth keeping. Anno made the enemies amorphous because the era's "enemy" was undefined [2]. Kin to Simple War: angels as lensed counterfactuals of a broken history (GDD 06 §6.6), humanity as latecomer, prophecy that fails.
2. **The Laundry Files** (Charles Stross, 2004–2026). Magic is a branch of applied computation; the occult is run by a civil-service bureaucracy with audits, treaties and software licensing. CASE NIGHTMARE GREEN is the slow-motion apocalypse of rising ambient magic. In *The Jennifer Morgue* a localised occult field makes reality bend toward a narrative archetype (a Bond-style hero gets the best odds) — the closest published kin to Simple War's Legend pressure. The series ends not with victory but with the Laundry dissolved and Britain surviving under a compromised "New Management": institutions stay human by bargaining, not winning [1].
3. **A Fire Upon the Deep** (Vernor Vinge, 1992). The galaxy's Zones of Thought change what physics and intelligence are possible; characters don't know whether the zones are natural or engineered — a reality-OS nobody fully understands. A human expedition awakens the Blight; fanatics conclude humans are its designed vector and destroy the largest human civilisation. The countermeasure that kills the Blight expands the Slow Zone, crashing thousands of civilisations and killing trillions. Humanity is the catalyst of both the apocalypse and a salvation that is itself a catastrophe [3].
4. **Mass Effect trilogy** (2007–2012). The Reapers harvest advanced life every 50,000 years; the Citadel and relay network are managed infrastructure that steers every civilisation down the same path — governance as trap. The Catalyst, built by the Leviathans to preserve organic life, concluded that harvesting species at their peak *is* preservation. Humanity is the latecomer that reaches the Catalyst and breaks the cycle; indoctrination is the enemy rewriting minds from inside. Lead writer Karpyshyn framed the Reapers as creator-and-destroyer gods [4].
5. **Halo** (2001–). The Forerunners held the Mantle (stewardship of life), failed against the Flood, and designated humanity "Reclaimers" through the Librarian's genetic geas; Forerunner systems answer only to human touch. The Covenant misread the Reclaimer glyph and launched a war of extermination to hide what it implied. Inherited infrastructure, a returning predator, and a latecomer species holding keys it never earned — with the war's consequences continuing across games rather than concluding [5][6].
6. **StarCraft / Brood War** (1998). Humanity is caught between two older powers and their creator race; Kerrigan's infestation and later de-infestation is the literal stay-human arc, and Brood War's alliances-of-convenience and betrayals (Raynor vowing to kill the Kerrigan he helped save) keep the question organisational as well as personal [7][8].
7. **Frostpunk** (2018). Survival city-builder where the second system underneath the heat economy is a one-way Book of Laws: child labour, sawdust in food, Faith or Order. Hope and discontent are lose-conditions equal to coal. The ending narrates back what kind of city you built, without scoring it. Staying human is a sequence of irreversible choices, not a speech [10][11].
8. **Spec Ops: The Line** (2012). A military shooter built to contextualise its own core mechanic: Walt Williams' GDC 2013 talk describes crafting story, themes and choices around shooting, with the protagonist sliding from professional to unhinged as the combat escalates. The stay-human question is asked of the player's hands, not just the script [9].

### Q2 — Tutorials and prologues for an evacuation world

- **StarCraft II, Mar Sara arc** (*Liberation Day* → *The Outlaws* → *Zero Hour*). The campaign opens in Joeyray's Bar with a TV newscast and a small raid on a Dominion logistics HQ — low stakes, familiar conflict, optional objectives (holoboards) teaching exploration. Then the Zerg return and Raynor arranges the planet's evacuation to the Hyperion. What worked: competence is built in a context the player understands, then the real threat arrives and every taught skill transfers; the evacuation is the payoff of the tutorial, not its premise. The later colonist arc (Dr. Hanson) turns evacuation itself into a choice between saving colonists and allowing their purification, with choice-linked alternate missions [8][12].
- **Halo: Reach, *New Alexandria***. The whole campaign is a losing evacuation; this level makes it mechanical. Three comm jammers can be done in any order; between them, randomly pooled emergency missions: escort Buck's Falcon, defend pinned troopers, and — the direct Angelfall analogue — destroy Shade turrets so Pelicans carrying civilians can lift off. Each turret cleared releases a shuttle. Designer Dan Miller called it a "super epic multi-parallel script". After the city is saved, Kat is shot mid-sentence, instantly, and dragged into a bunker; the cost lands without fanfare. What worked: evacuation as playable texture, randomised order for replayability, and grief delivered in gameplay's own register [6].
- **Mass Effect 1, Eden Prime; Mass Effect 3, Earth**. ME1's first mission is routine colony aid that reveals the cosmic war (Sovereign lifting off, the beacon); ME3 opens with Earth already falling. Two prologue shapes: the small mission that cracks open, and in medias res collapse [4].
- **Frostpunk, A New Home**. Onboarding is crisis-driven: when food runs out or hands run short, the game suggests the relevant law. The tutorial is the first legislation; the generator is the single organising constraint everything else hangs from [10][11].
- **The Jeff archetype**. Murray Bauman (Stranger Things) is the cleanest model: a disgraced journalist whose conspiracy frame (a Russian invasion of Hawkins) is wrong in its specifics but right that something is being covered up; dismissed by the sheriff, he becomes a recurring, trusted ally with concrete skills [13]. The pattern: give the theorist one verifiable hit early, keep the rest of his frame wrong, let institutions dismiss him, and make believing him a relationship choice. Reach's shell-shocked trooper is a darker variant: his rant about being turned into the enemy is wrong in detail but rhymes with the Flood's actual horror — madness as distorted truth [6].

### Q3 — Time-linear campaigns, faction arcs, recurring NPCs

- **Brood War** runs three chronological episodes (Protoss evacuation from Aiur → UED invasion → Kerrigan's ascendancy), starting two days after StarCraft ended. The player is an anonymous commander per faction; named NPCs recur and shift allegiance across episodes — Raynor, Kerrigan, Mengsk, Zeratul, Fenix, Duran. Aldaris's uprising is recontextualised two episodes later; Duran's betrayals tie all three campaigns together. Story is layered: manual, mission briefings, in-mission conversations, end-of-campaign cinematics. Producer Shane Dabiri's stated goal was objectives that reflect the story ("Final Fantasy type events"), fewer pure-annihilation missions [7].
- **StarCraft II** keeps one faction per release in chronological order, but Wings of Liberty lets players take missions in any order while the storyline outcome stays fixed; the Hyperion hub carries character continuity between missions, and Zeratul's prophecy crosses faction lines into the other campaigns [8].
- **Warcraft III** tells one war through four campaigns in fixed order (human → undead → orc → night elf), each recontextualising the last, converging on the Burning Legion as the common enemy that forces former foes to stand together [14]. This is the closest published shape to "three splinters plus a raid-boss-tier power": separate arcs, shared events, one convergence.
- **Halo** keeps the mainline chronological and places prequels (Reach) to deepen rather than reorder; the Reclaimer/Mantle thread recurs across a hundred millennia of backstory, and the story never concludes [5][6].
- **Fit for Simple War**: the three splinters share a core roster (GDD 06 §6.7), so SC2-style arcs with a shared hub and recurring cast (Jeff, the planetary AI, named Lawbringers) fit better than three separate games. Brood War-style interludes — missions where the player commands a different splinter, or where splinters ally and betray — carry the cross-faction plot. Lawbringers as raid-boss-tier commanders with few auxiliaries (GDD §6.7) map onto the late-campaign Protoss ally or the Mount Hyjal convergence; the True Believers antagonist using Lawbringer assets maps onto Duran.

### Q4 — Pitfalls and how the best works kept depth

- **Shallow stay-human themes.** Frostpunk never moralises directly: the law book and the closing report are the only commentary. Spec Ops ties the theme to the core mechanic instead of dialogue. The cautionary tale is Mass Effect 3's ending: a late, exposition-heavy Catalyst speech recontextualising the whole war split the fanbase (the "indoctrination theory" arose partly as coping). Lesson: distribute the theme across pressured choices from mission one; never deliver it as a final sermon [4][9][10][11].
- **Lore dumps.** Dark Souls delivers lore through three optional channels — brief NPC dialogue, item descriptions read during ordinary inventory management, and world placement (the legendary archer's bow found on a corpse in the ruin he entered). Battey's critique of in-game textbooks and audio logs: players must sit still to consume them, so most won't. Brood War layered manual/briefing/dialogue for different depths. The risk of pure opt-in: players assume there is no story — mitigate with one diegetic summariser (Jeff) [7][12].
- **Chosen-one clichés.** Dune Messiah exists to expose the cost of the messiah the first book let readers cheer; criticism reads the saga as a deconstruction of prophecy-driven heroism [15]. A Fire Upon the Deep makes humanity the suspected *vector* of the apocalypse; Evangelion makes humanity the accidental usurper. Simple War's catalyst framing should carry blame and cost, not destiny: humanity's intervention kills magic and calls back predators (GDD §6.1). The player is a self-insert commander, and Jeff is right about exactly one thing — "absurd options can be right" without anyone being anointed [3][2].
- **Tonal whiplash.** Wings of Liberty reviewers flagged flat dialogue and early missions lacking urgency despite civilisation-level stakes — the bar humour and the genocide never quite met. Reach holds one elegiac register throughout, with humour as brief human texture; Frostpunk permits relief but never breaks its grimness. Jeff's comedy should be coping under dread, and escalation should track the Law's fraying, not the writer's mood [8][6][10].

## Relevance to Simple War

- **Vision (GDD 01)**: story campaigns are the main draw (R1Q2); pillar 4 ("legendary results from a fraying reality") has a direct kin in the Laundry's narrative-bending fields [1]; pillar 3 (hoverable defined terms, Glossary doc 07) is the native lore channel the Dark Souls analysis recommends [12]. Angelfall is release step 2, reworked with editor tools — this report feeds that rework and the narrative pass (open topic 3).
- **Narrative Bible (GDD 06)**: §6.1's core theme (staying human when defeat erases the meaning of "human") is exactly Frostpunk's and Spec Ops' question, and §6.1's "deeper than shallow themes" (R4Q23) is satisfied by their methods, not by more dialogue. §6.2's magic-as-OS with registry, audits, Ruin-as-500-errors is the Laundry's computation-magic plus Vinge's zones. §6.6's angels as lensed counterfactuals mirrors Evangelion's alternate-humanity messengers. §6.9's tutorial already has the New Alexandria shape: hangar zones as shuttle LZs, CR-0 robots as guards that can't board, blocking Angels as the turrets that must be cleared — and Jeff's late-game line ("angels almost never show up to a battle they can't lose") unlocking the inverted auto-resolve is a textbook relationship-gated payoff of the Bauman pattern [6][13].
- **Factions (§6.7)**: Humanity First's hardship choices, CHI's bonus-objective attention splits and the Enlightened's warlord fantasy are three different moral loops over one roster — Frostpunk's scenario-recontextualisation trick (same systems, different uncomfortable question) is the cheap way to make each splinter campaign feel distinct [11].

## Options and recommendations

1. **Tutorial spine — escalate from small stakes (recommended).** Open on Jeff's absurdly specific local problem (one town, one blockade), teach routing and escort there, then let the Angelfall widen to the spaceport. Trade-off: slower start than in medias res; mitigated by the turn-4 second blockade already in the slice. Alternative: Reach-style cold open mid-evacuation (immediate dread, but mechanics taught under fire); Frostpunk-style crisis-suggested actions (elegant, but risks feeling like a systems demo).
2. **Jeff — Bauman model with a relationship gate (recommended; already GDD canon).** One early verifiable hit, everything else wrong, institutions dismiss him, mishandling sends him into seclusion. Trade-off: comedy can undercut dread — keep his wrongness concrete and his fear visible. Avoid making him an oracle (kills the theme) or pure comic relief (wastes the pattern).
3. **Campaign architecture — chronological spine, optional order, convergence finale (recommended).** One splinter per arc, shared hub and recurring cast, Brood War-style interludes for cross-splinter beats, Lawbringers arriving late as raid-boss allies against a True Believer/Duran-style betrayal. Trade-off: more scripting than strict linear episodes; the faction-order-independent state machine stays a stretch goal (GDD §6.1). Strict BW linearity is cheaper but brittle for a self-insert commander who may be cast as several commanders (R1Q10).
4. **Theme delivery — systemic choices plus retrospective judgement (recommended).** Hardship choices with irreversible consequences, and an end-of-campaign report that states what the player's command became, without scoring it. Trade-off: needs sim-side consequence tracking (dispositions already live in the sim, §6.8); dialogue-only delivery is cheaper but lands shallow; pure Dark Souls silence risks players missing the theme entirely.
5. **Lore channels — tooltips, unit descriptions, optional hub scenes (recommended).** Glossary tooltips as the primary layer, flavour on units/attacks as the second, optional between-mission scenes as the third, Jeff as diegetic summariser. Trade-off: opt-in depth means some players see none of it — accept that, per Dark Souls, rather than force cutscenes.
