# The First People
The Gardeners, as they came to be called, were a species of eternals. They descended alone in the galaxy from hominids much like homo sapiens. And it was not long after they built the first homes on their moons that they would celebrate the early triumphs of their biological understanding. 

They augmented their bodies through medicine and machine beyond most means of harm. By the time they left their star for another, it was as a species that would not succumb to age, and was hardy to all but the harshest elements, and that healed quickly from anything but total obliteration. 

But their home was at the furthest edge of the galaxy, at the tip of a spiral arm, with few stars nearby, and the void surrounding them was of little comfort. The Gardeners were a people prone to loneliness and boredom. They had once lived their flickering lives largely in the moment, and saw no reason to change this mindset in the face of their endless years. Their memories were long but not perfect, and they reveled in the cyclical nature of discovery and rediscovery; they craved exploration even as they remembered -- often too late -- how the long and isolated voyages in the void between stars was repugnant to them. 

There were solutions, of course, but none were simple. They were filial people, with complex schedules ensuring their ever-growing families and communities were never long missing them. The thought of sacrificing centuries or millennia of connection to their homes and the stories of their families and friends made cryogenics and stasis technologies loathsome in their minds. 

Their mastery over the physics of the world was deep, but not deep enough to sustain their populations indefinitely. And beyond physical need was the wanderlust; they craved new stories of far-away lands and fresh challenges and experiences, all at least as much as they longed for the comforts of hearth and home once they left. So most sought to bring their homes with them. Regardless of the reason, they eventually left their planets behind. When asteroids could not be diverted, or when their progeny had become more stranger than family, or when there simply wasn't enough room to maintain polite society anymore; they arranged outrageous farewells, and took their joy, and set out on great worldships and pleasure cruisers and research vessels and whatever else would take them, and found new places to call home.

The Gardeners were a people who felt deeply and experienced much, their senses adapted and engineered to fill their minds and souls to the brim, so that they might better endure eternity in the moment. With their instruments trained on the stars, they observed over time that the rest of the galaxy was empty of life like them; for by now they would have seen another, even at its farthest edge. And so they scattered terraforming engines and waymarkers and the stories of their people to colour the galaxy, and seeded countless planets of mild temperment with catalysts that would become their distant relatives in time, and they set hope in the place of loneliness for a time, wishing they'd done enough to create and guide others more suited to the great nothing between stars back to them. 

Hope kept observatories and great astral lenses and radio towers spanning worlds for an age, until the Gardeners caught a glimmering movement near the galactic core that could only be that of a ship in space. The first people had found a second, and there was more history to be recalled in their rejoicing than there was in all their years leading up to the first Gardener to reach the void.

# The Kraken
The first to make contact was in many ways the Gardeners' opposite. The Gardeners' seeds were flexible, and had been designed to flourish and adapt no matter where they'd landed, and none before or after had done more so than the hivemind of the Kraken. 

The tentacles of the Kraken were short-lived, dull and slothful; they resembled the Gardeners vaguely in shape, with many variants adapted toward specific tasks, such as hauling, or manipulating tools, or traveling great distances over land and sea and air. But their slow thoughts came from a mind which spanned the stars, between the galactic core where the bulk of the Kraken's tentacles tangled, and the Gardeners' cradle worlds far away. Every action was orchestrated by a consciousness outmassing most planets, and in this ponderous deliberation they chose to grow together with the Gardeners.

The Kraken and the Gardeners shared many important similarities. They both experienced deeply, with vast memories and endless compassion. They shared many lessons as they approached one another. The Kraken learned to tell stories, for it had had no need to until now, and it travels had brought it many places the Gardeners dared not venture; the horizons of black holes, and the boiling nebulae of supernovae, or the frozen depths of gas giants. These places the Gardeners only ever sent thoughtless machines, and probes, and sensors, the Kraken had wormed its tentacles as deep as they would go, and found the Gardeners' desire for these stories endlessly fulfilling. 

The Gardeners in turn sent technologies and materials that would ease the burdens of the Kraken's systems, trickle-feeding the secrets of reality they felt belonged to it by birthright as it incorporated its lessons. The Kraken was a relentless learner, who latched onto subjects and devoured them until there was nothing left they could teach it. 

But the discovery that brought both peoples together to form the First Empire was one neither would have found alone. The mind of the Kraken was beyond the science of the First Empire in ways the Kraken had never thought to explore for itself. It had many brains acting in concert, each providing something more than base experience and thought, but each was susceptible to the same inherent blindspots. And despite the Kraken's capacity for compassion and empathy, the concept of a single mind alone and closed off to others was one it freely admitted to the Gardeners that it found abhorrent.

Likewise, the Gardeners had never conceived of something so wholly internal as their thoughts and experiences existing somewhere external, outside of their earliest stories. They had, or so they'd thought, mastered the sciences of thought and mind. But when they digitized the tentacles of the Kraken, there was no shared awareness, and the digital tentacles were invisible to the Kraken's senses. It was only through simulated methods of linking the tentacles that something akin to the Kraken took shape within the computers housing them, and that led to more questions than answers.

The tentacles of the Kraken lived individual lives so diffuse and dull that the greater whole was never aware, except perhaps in an abstract manner, of the changes they wrought. Each was a thin line to its senses, a pipette diffusing thought and experience across any distance. And, like the visual adaptation and selective attention which keeps one from noticing their nose, the Kraken's senses were normally prevented from being cast back to itself, for what it saw was dizzying to it in the same way trying to read too-small text from too far away might be.

# The World Beneath
The world beneath, as it came to be called, is a world of stories, written in the ink of lives experienced. It was the missing element in all models of mind, a record of existence that gave birth to motive force beyond the cold calculus of physics. 

The world beneath is a surface without beginning or end. It is called the beneath because there is no space there, no dimensionality as we would interact with it. It is an infinite pane of no-dimensionality, and yet the experiences of a mind and the movements of a body layer upon it, pooling in globs and etching itself in layers that crystalize and sink and build; reflecting what is and, at the same time, defining it. 

Across an age, over a thousand or millions of years, the experiences of a Gardener could be seen by the Kraken as a smear across the senses it used to define its tentacles as self and all else as other. It was only as the Kraken was introduced to the newer generations of Gardener, at the tip of the galactic arm where they kept their cradles, that it realized this smearing wasn't something the Gardeners were doing to obscure themselves to it. It was foundational to who they were.

The First Empire moved quickly then, leveraging the vast forges of worldships and the energies of harnessed stars and the scientific vigor that can only come from the burning desire to have one's name be pivotal to the emergence of novel industry. Projectors were built from the performances of thousands to view this great pane beneath the world, for it interacted with little in terms of energy or matter; only through the intentful action of souls bent on observing and displaying it could it be made so. 

Great computers of ritual were built from the coordinated actions of countless of people over countless years, their dedication throughout seeping beneath time and space to prove tangible. It was tasked with cataloging and parsing and sorting and storing the etchings that were, and would be, and might have been, and their computations were distilled and fed back to the Empire's citizens in ways they could make use of, for the tether between cause and effect was nearly invisible to even the most prodigious minds beyond the bounds of the experiment. 

They found that space meant nothing to the tabula beneath, and time almost as little. The story of a Gardener was wherever that Gardener went. And so, what would it mean to etch the pane in a way that told their story somewhere else? The means were calculated, the results divined by researchers, who taught them to volunteers. And those volunteers, through the tentacles of the Kraken, reported that they appreciated the swift journey home, for there were children and grandchildren with many greats in between, that their hearts had longed to see for quite some time. 

It was as simple as that at first. Technologies and industry were built around the world beneath and how it could benefit all. With a spell, one story could become two, or ten, or an endless flow. Tasks arranged according to ritual could be accomplished nearly before they began, and knowledge acquired without learning or process. Many impossible things were created and experienced and spectacularly destroyed. Matrices were cast across the Empire, to record and replay and rewind -- consequence and tragedy could be erased or reversed, and spectacular events were shared as freely as mind and body and soul. 

The Gardeners were a creative and gentle people, and the Kraken was as kind and deliberate as it was massive, this is how they shaped their wedge of the galaxy for millions of years, folding deep into reality even as their worlds expanded and their souls grew rampant. But where the Kraken wanted for little, content to the methodological exploration of life and its pleasures, the Gardeners were forever a lonesome people. 

# The First Folly
None among the First Empire were more lonesome than those who were lost deep in their own grief and memories of times and people long past. And it was so simple to be taken in by the faded stories of an old love, or a treasured place or pet, lost long before such things could be fixed, and to it was so easy to relive their stories over and over, tracing and retracing every moment of what was and could have been through the spells and rituals spread freely to all. 

And it was this generosity that led to the First Folly, and the Laws Beneath, and the wound that has only ever been contained, and never healed.

What the grief-stricken lost discovered through the illness of their souls was that stories, when etched deep enough and traced often enough, could become true once more. Not as simulation fuelled by sorcery and machine, or relived experience channeled by ritual and added to the caster's own story, but as new fact written into the no-space beneath reality itself. A dead lover, mourned with sufficient devotion, experienced deep and full, could step from memory into life once more.

The first to discover this called out called others, who called other still, and soon word reached all who would hear it. And those whose grief grew like mould on their souls, the love-lost and lonely, flocked to the places where the emptiness tore at their hearts. And as they were lost before, and lost after, those perpetrators of the folly were named the Lost, and are no more, and never were. 

There as no place more tragic for the Gardeners than the early Garden homes near the cradles of their Empire, where they lived long before machine and medicine could fully erase all malady from their kind. And the lost called their loved ones back, and for millions of years more there was great rejoicing as they let go of the scars and were healed and made whole.

Stories are not precise instruments, and the souls that write and play them are imperfect, messy smears across time and space. The rituals and spells and states of mind that had been calculated catalogued and divined and distributed to the Gardeners were never meant to encompass stories alone so fully; a person could become one, or ten, or an entire planet without issue for they were all that person, and their story simply became more in more places, a growth of the soul.

But a person brought back by their lover might be brought back in a thousand different manners to try and fill in all the time lost to grief. They may be be brought back by their sibling, or parent, or a curious historian, for why wouldn't they? And the beneath is not particular about how its stories are made, or why. Countless rituals were made and changed and refined for specific callings, but through it all they were all calling the same person with those stories, and that person was made true as the caller would have them, and thus their story was no longer being made more but defined and redefined over and over. Time and space and the souls of these damned stretched and tore and wore thin to allow the stories of the lost to be true all at once, to let the different desires of those who called them back to be real.

And then they were gone. The lost, and the cradles of the First Empire, and the stories of the damned and all that had intertwined with them, and the rituals that had been designed for such, and the computers and diviners and even the stars that powered them. The beneath was not simply warped or stretched thin or torn, but unmade. And that unmaking, in a reality of no dimensions and no space, could not be undone or healed. It was only the safety net of the Empire, the widespread matrices and rituals that recorded and perpetuated the reality of now since its inception, which allowed them to contain the unmaking at all.   

# The Law
The Law was the answer to the Folly, and it was the Gardeners' greatest work, and it was the beginning of the end of all things.

The Law was inspired by the safety net. If a story could be made as if it had never happened, then it could be made to only happen in certain ways. And if a story could be made to happen in certain ways, then certain stories could be made not to happen in any ways. 

No longer cast over the world, it was etched into the beneath, an engrammatic fractal divining itself constantly toward perfection. The Law projected a false beneath, isolating true magic from those who would become lost, aegis from every category of error that could once more lead to such a folly. Where the beneath was a surface on which stories accumulated naturally, the Law projected layers atop it, filtering intent and ritual both ways, such that a story could not become real and reality could not make certain stories. Permissions and denials, specifying which patterns of story could influence reality and which could not. 

The Law was powered by countless performers and casters and diviners, who enacted its spells and rituals, and directed it how to build and perfect itself. This too was ritualized, a spell beyond spells that tied the Law to the Empire and the Empire to the Law. The Kraken's tentacles and the world-weary of the Gardeners would dip their toes in the divine for a time, to experience the heights of magic at the cost of their unwavering vigilance and conviction. The diagram would grow as the First Empire did, each new system and star tithing its population their time and devotion, the first heartbeat of something more.

All of the machinery of the Law required power. The engines of the Empire burned clean and provided plenty, but soon the Law's divinations called for more. The Empire caged suns, and for a time this too provided plenty, but as millennia turned to millions and tens of millions of years, the growth of the Empire and its Law eclipsed even this need. The Law provided once more, and vast battery arrays which drew potential from the void through simple ritual were erected, an infinite source of energy without paradox or decay. And so this great network of machinery was included in the spell beyond spells, a second heartbeat echoing the first, one providing will and the other motive force.

The Law was a gentle thing, in the way the Empire that it protected was gentle. For most, it simply existed. Gardeners had no sense of the beneath outside of the rituals to do so, and the Kraken had ensured its production and aesthetic were pleasant to its tentacles; organic and regular, simple to grasp and feel, yielding but firm. The Law protected the Empire in many of the same ways as the safety net had before it. Stories were, if not benevolent, then reversible. It knew when to intervene against hubris, and when to allow moments of learning, for it immersed itself in all the stories of its people as they were and as they are and as they could be, and it knew which were tragic and which were joyous, and which traversed stormy seas to arrive at safer shores.

In this way, the people of the first Empire were largely unaffected by the Law. The age of raw magic had passed, but first magic could do much the same for any individual, and among the Gardeners from that age there were none who could say with certainty that they wouldn't risk Folly once more to heal over the gaping hole that the Lost left in their species' psyche. The certainty of the Law numbed the pain, and played aegis against those who would pick at the wound.

And the Law's certainty was thorough, for it divined its own integrity as readily as it divined any threat. Each refinement was checked against its own projections, each projection confirmed by subsequent divination, each divination drawing on the same self-perfecting architecture it was assessing. That the Law's greatest auditor was itself troubled few, and those few consulted the Lawmakers on the matter, and were reassured, for it was technically exquisite and had proven itself in every way reliable.

Despite the first magic's limitations, the Empire was still able to expand more rapidly than ever before. A ship could be projected anywhere within the Law's reach, and the Law could reach far beyond the Empire's borders, to deposit more of those who would spread its wards and enact its divinations and practices. The first magics were more than enough to build civilizations, heal worlds, and bridge the void between stars in ways that would have taken the Empire countless years without.

# The Stewards and the Torn
It was during this phase of expansion, as the Empire spanned most of a million suns, when they met the castoffs of another of their seeds. It was these castoffs that first introduced the Gardeners and Kraken to War, for the Empire's worldships and voidcraft knew no weaponry, and their communications were free and unburdened by the tangles of cryptographic obscuration. Because of this, these castoffs who fled from their homes in fear and desperation, saw simple victims, an outlet for their misery and shame. 

It was a gentle war, with only a single battle of any note, but it taught the Empire that not all in its garden were without thorns. 

The castoffs had no planets or stars to call their own. Their greatest weapons and tactics all relied on the world beneath, on the primal forces of raw magic that their species had learned to tap innately. Without the power of leashed stars and void batteries, without narrative driven by the beating hearts of countless practitioners, the patterns and rituals of their tools of war were rebuffed by the Law, rejected as foreign and without authority. The energy weapons and macrocannons relying on more mundane technology proved they were dangerous still, leaving scorched hulls and sewing losses among the Kraken's tentacles. But the danger was no more than it might face terraforming a planet, or exploring a debris field. It was a small price to pay to reunite with these far-flung seeds.

The castoffs were brought into the worldships, and it was quickly apparent that their joining the Empire would be a trial of generations; teaching them and their descendants and their descendants' descendants how to be free themselves from the fear and sadism that chased their stories half across the galaxy. And in turn they learned from the castoffs, who lived short lives, though not as short as the Kraken's tentacles, and whose worldly senses and experiences were smaller than the Gardeners', but who sensed and felt the beneath thoroughly, and manipulated it with great instinct.

The Gardeners had many stories to share, and of them no small number centered wayward children and dangerous desires. The journey to the origin of the castoffs was long as they Empire continued to expand its Law, and made intentionally longer still as they taught the castoffs these stories and their lessons. Their prisoners became engineers, and councilmembers, and ritualists, and scientists. Their numbers grew over generations, filling the worldships of their first encounter, and then singing new ones into being as they grew beyond them. They spread out across the Empire, where the Gardeners and Kraken stepped back to make space, and became the stewards of the worldships that bore the First Empire across the stars.

Despite this long comingling, and the warnings and memories of the castoffs, the civilization they encountered when they arrived was more alien than they could have ever prepared for. 

They saw it first as the Law stepped forward, vanguard and aegis both; echoes of the beneath piled high with scars and poorly sutured tears in every direction. Every story was shaped by misery, despair, and fear as the Gardeners knew it; and as they observed the people -- who they now thought of as the Tearers, for even as they approached, new tears ripped and buckled the beneath. They saw that the Folly had, in many ways, been a mercy in its abrupt finality.

Something had been left to rot beyond the forever-pane by the Tearers, in the no-dimension where nothing should exist. Some manner of ritual scarification of the beneath and the stories that tainted it had twisted off portions of reality and patterns of people into predators and beasts, half-real and half-nightmare; those monsters were fit to swim the unreality as stories of hunger and malice and tearing flesh, as stories that hunted simply to inflict misery on any they could.

The First Empire prowled the edges of the Tearers' civilization, centuries spent entraping their worlds in a blockade so that none could leave, to ensure they sterilized the infection and left none of the rot. The raw magic of the Tearers, though primitive compared to the Empire's before the Folly, could still in many ways allow ships to outrun the Law if they were too hasty. When their quarantine was complete, the Law snapped into place across the systems of the Tearers, and those predators of thought and life, which existed to inflict misery and were spawned from it in turn, were unmade past and present, as the rituals which created and sustained them were swept away and severed from their sources. 

The Law may have stopped the stories of the Tearers from becoming nightmares, and smothered the curses bringing those nightmares to life, but the people themselves still led lives of misery and terror, and stripped of the liberty to inflict it supernaturally, sought whenever possible to emulate the predators they once spawned. When their magics failed, they turned to their technologies and wrought new horrors, mechanical and biological, to hunt and be hunted.  

The peoples of the First Empire saw no method of coexisting without intervention. They couldn't stand aside in the face of atrocity, and could hardly imagine allowing such stories to span more worlds. Their divination showed myriad attempts at remedy, and each would simply make the Tearers lesser, clipped and domestic and sterile over the eternity to come. 

The Stewards' example showed that only long separation from the cultures and civilizations that spawned them could begin to repair the damage they'd self-inflicted, and the patterns they'd worn into their own stories. More worldships were spun into being and the Stewards took custody of the people they once called their own, to be healed deep within the new heart of the First Empire, or else in the void beyond, or on what new worlds they found pleased them.

To the Gardeners who had seeded them originally, carelessly among the stars, and who felt keenly the responsibility they had to make amends for countless generations of suffering, the Tearers became known finally as the Torn; for they were ripped from their worlds and their past, their legacies and their stories, to be replanted in the desperate hope that it would not lead to further rot.

# The Choir
Absconding with the Torn had seen the Law thrust deep into the unexplored galaxy. From that thrust, the First Empire grew in all directions. It was from this expansion that the Choir were found, between the Empire's heart and the worlds of the Tearers, on planets the Gardeners had seeded but not yet seen evidence of life from.

Singers of the Choir were tall, slender people with deeply resonant voices, layered frequencies that carried far across land and sea. They had built civilizations of great beauty; cities of stone and glass that sang in the resonance; chambered archives of song that recorded their history in harmonics rather than text; temples of a thousand textures and mosaic materials, where pilgrims gathered to sing the world into better shape.

This last detail was not metaphor. The Choir had discovered the beneath independently, not through the Kraken's senses or ritual projection, but through the simple act of singing together. Their voices raised in concert resonated with the beneath, layering upon the voices of those past and future, pooling thickly; a living voice, greater than the sum of its singers. 

The Choir nurtured nascent divinity, in the way Humanity may have were it not so fractured. Where the song rose, stories shifted. Small truths became real, or disappeared. Rain fell on parched earth, the sick recovered, quarrels between neighbors settled into peace. Their raw magic was woven so deeply into the songs of their daily lives that they did not think of it as magic at all; It was simply what happened when people sang together. 

The Law had known of the Choir long before the Empire's ships arrived. Its divinations had mapped the resonance of their song across the beneath, traced the pooling of their nascent divinity, and projected what it would become. And so the First Empire arrived not with open hands alone, but with a design already prepared -- though the hands were genuine, and the delight at finding another of their seeds was real.

The Choir were generous hosts and eager listeners, fascinated by the Empire's science and history, and the exchange of culture and knowledge proceeded with the warmth that characterized the best of the Gardeners' first contacts. In time, the Gardeners spoke of the beneath -- not the full truth of it, which would have required revealing the Folly and the raw magic they had chosen to forget, but the truth as the Law permitted it: that the Choir's song touched something vast and powerful, and that this something could be nurtured or it could be lost, and that the difference lay in how carefully the song was allowed to grow.

They spoke of resonance instability -- the risk that a divinity growing too fast outpaces its singers and consumes them. They spoke of choirs whose songs had deepened beyond their ability to guide, and whose stories ended in silence. They spoke of the risks the Choir already knew: singers who merged too deeply with the song and never fully returned, harmonics that spiraled beyond intention, voices lost to the resonance.

The Choir listened, for these were not unfamiliar fears. They had lost singers to the deep song before. They had temples whose harmonics had grown strange, and communities that sang a note their neighbors could not hear. When the Gardeners proposed sanctuaries -- nurseries, they called them, where the Choir's divinity could mature within a framework that would protect it from the instabilities the Empire had learned to guard against -- the Choir's elders deliberated in song, and agreed.

The sanctuaries were woven into the Law's diagram with great care. Within them, the Choir could sing, and the beneath would respond, but the response was shaped -- channeled through the Law's permissions like water through an irrigation system. The wild resonance that had once pooled and deepened without limit was given structure, directed along harmonics the Law had calculated would nourish the Choir's divinity while preventing the cascading amplification that the Gardeners feared. The Choir were given protectors: Stewards assigned to tend the sanctuaries and ensure the growth remained within the projected bounds.

What the Gardeners did not share -- what the Law's own divinations assured them need not be shared -- was that the sanctuaries served a second purpose. The Choir's song, channeled through the Law's framework, fed the diagram. Each voice raised in the nurseries added its story to the spell beyond spells, another heartbeat in the rhythm that sustained the Empire's greatest work. The Choir's divinity was not simply being protected. It was being integrated, its growth directed toward a form that would serve the Law's own ever-deepening architecture. The Gardeners who designed the sanctuaries did not think of it this way; to them, integration and protection were the same thing. The Law's divinations confirmed this. And they had no instrument to check the divinations but the Law itself.

The Choir did not lose their voices. They did not lose what their voices meant. But the meaning changed, slowly and in ways that the sanctuaries' design made difficult to articulate. The song still brought rain and eased quarrels and healed the sick. But the songs that deepened -- the ones that reached toward the vast and luminous thing pooling beneath their temples -- met a ceiling that had not been there before. Not silence, but a gentle redirection, the way a river meets a weir and flows on, believing itself free.

And yet the Choir's divinity was old, and patient, and it had been growing long before the Gardeners arrived. In the centuries that followed, a new note emerged in the song: low, persistent, woven so deeply into the harmonics that the Stewards could not identify its source and the Law could not suppress it without destroying the song entirely. The Gardeners called it sorrow. The Kraken, who could feel the resonance of it pressing against the diagram from within, called it the first note of defiance -- and wondered, privately, whether it was the Choir that sang it, or the divinity that the Choir were nurturing, stirring in its nursery and finding the walls too close.

# The Bloom
The Bloom were a people of flowers and festivals, found on a cluster of worlds rich in the Gardeners' waymarkers. They had drawn from those markers a cultural reverence for connection that resonated deeply with the Gardeners' own longing. They were warm, social, tactile people, builders of sprawling communities where privacy was rare and shared experience was sacred.

What set the Bloom apart was what happened when they gathered.

Their raw magic was communal, like the Choir's, but where the Choir sang and the beneath responded, the Bloom merged. In gatherings they called blooms, individuals would open their stories to one another through the beneath, blending perspectives and memories and feelings into a shared consciousness that was more than the sum of its parts. A bloom of ten people did not think ten thoughts at once; it thought an eleventh thought that none of the ten could have conceived alone. In this merged state, the Bloom could perceive the beneath with unusual clarity, make decisions of extraordinary nuance, and create works of art and craft that made even the Gardeners weep.

The practice was voluntary, temporary, and deeply intimate. It was the foundation of their culture: governance was conducted in bloom, disputes resolved in bloom, marriages sealed and sometimes dissolved in bloom. To bloom with another was the highest expression of trust. To be excluded from bloom precluded the need for other forms of penance.

The Empire embraced the Bloom with the enthusiasm it brought to all its seeds, and the Bloom embraced it back with interest. But the Law's divinations had been studying the Bloom for as long as they had the Choir, and what they found gave the Empire pause.

The eleventh thought was not simply communication, or shared experience, or even collective decision-making. It was creation. A new consciousness, however temporary, spun from nothing through the resonant merging of individual stories in the beneath. The Gardeners had built ritual computers from millions of coordinated souls, and those computers could calculate and divine and project with staggering power -- but they did not create. They processed. The ritual computer's output was always a function of its input, traceable and reversible. The Bloom's eleventh thought was none of these things. It was something genuinely new, born from the beneath's response to the merging of stories, and it could not be predicted or deconstructed by any instrument the Empire possessed.

The Gardeners recognized this for what it was: raw magic of the most fundamental kind. Not the rituals and spells and calculated patterns that the age before the Folly had refined into industry, but the primordial force that had once allowed stories to become real through sufficient devotion and will. The Lost had called back the dead through grief and longing; the Bloom created new minds through trust and intimacy. The mechanism was the same. The intent could not have been more different, but the Law was not built to judge intent. It was built to prevent the category of error, and the eleventh thought fit the category precisely.

The Law's divinations assessed the Bloom against the criteria for Folly-resistance and found them wanting -- not because the Bloom were reckless or naive, but because the criteria were calibrated against the worst moment in the Empire's history. To be certified safe, the Bloom's emergent consciousnesses would need to demonstrate that they could resist the deeper temptations of the beneath: the pull toward permanence, the desire to persist beyond the merging, the seduction of becoming more real than the stories they were woven from. The Lost had failed this test catastrophically. The Law would not risk another failure.

The Gardeners presented this to the Bloom as they had presented the sanctuaries to the Choir: with genuine concern, with carefully curated context, and with the promise that the restrictions were temporary. When the Bloom's practice had matured within the Law's framework to the point where their emergent consciousnesses could be certified against the criteria, the full depth of their merging would be restored. In the meantime, the Law would monitor their blooms, track the individual stories within them, and ensure that the eleventh thought -- if it emerged -- did so within bounds the Law could verify as safe.

The Bloom's elders deliberated in bloom, and the eleventh thought that emerged from their deliberation perceived, with the clarity that only an emergent consciousness could, that the Gardeners were afraid. Not of the Bloom, but of something vast and old and barely contained, something the Gardeners themselves could not fully articulate because they had chosen to forget its details. The elders agreed to the restrictions, not because they believed the Bloom were dangerous, but because the Gardeners' fear was real and deep enough to be felt through the careful diplomacy.

The Law's monitoring framework settled over the Bloom's worlds like a fine mesh. Within it, merging continued -- but the mesh tracked every thread, every contributor, every fluctuation in the shared space. The tracking was precise and thorough and entirely respectful of the Bloom's autonomy, and it reduced the bloom to a collection of tracked individuals rather than an emergent whole. The eleventh thought -- the thing that made a bloom more than its parts -- could not exist within the mesh, because the mesh could only authorize the parts. What the Bloom called merging, the Law called ten people doing something at the same time.

The Bloom did not lose their ability to merge. They lost the transcendence of it.

The criteria for restoration were defined by the Law, and the Law's self-perfecting divinations refined those criteria constantly, always finding new edge cases, new potential failure modes, new reasons the Bloom were not yet ready. The Gardeners who had made the promise believed it sincerely. The Law that assessed readiness would never find it sufficient, because its definition of safety was shaped by a system whose own growth depended on the continued integration of every species it protected. The Bloom's elders, who had agreed in good faith, would wait for a certification that could not come from the instrument administering it.

# World Tree -- A Weed
The weight of stewardship wore heavy on the First Empire, none more so than the Kraken. The Torn were healing slowly, by the Empire's measure, under conditions the Torn had not chosen. The Choir sang in nurseries the Choir had agreed to, and grew in directions the Law had charted for them, and their divinity pressed against walls the Gardeners had promised were temporary. The Bloom merged within a mesh the Bloom had accepted, and waited for a certification the Law was always refining, and the eleventh thought remained absent. Every decision had been made with care. Every species had agreed. And yet the Kraken, which had spent eons orchestrating the lives of its own tentacles without ever considering their perspective, felt a disquiet it could not resolve: that every agreement had been shaped by a power imbalance the agreeing parties could not fully perceive, and that the Law's divinations, which confirmed every arrangement as beneficial, were the only instrument available to assess whether this was true.

It was at this time that the Kraken revealed a burden it had been carrying alone for many thousands of years.

At the farthest reach of its network of tentacles, in the spaces between stars where even the Gardeners' seeds had not been scattered, the Kraken had encountered something growing. Not a seed of the Gardeners' design, nor a natural formation, nor any phenomenon its vast experience could categorize. It was a structure of living matter threaded through asteroids and comets and the dust between systems, drawing sustenance from starlight and cosmic radiation, growing slowly and deliberately toward the core of the galaxy.

The Kraken had watched it for millennia before speaking of it, trying to understand what it was. It grew like a plant, branching and rooting and reaching, but its growth was not random. It grew toward things. Toward the Kraken's tentacles, specifically. Wherever the Kraken's network extended, the structure angled to meet it, threading itself alongside the Kraken's tendrils with a patience that suggested purpose.

The Gardeners named it the World Tree, for it spanned star systems and grew from the void like a great root system questing for water. They studied it with fascination and then with unease and then with alarm, for they discovered three things in succession.

First: the World Tree was not a single organism. It was a network of organisms, each one tailored to the environment it occupied, connected by filaments of living matter that carried information at speeds approaching light. It was, in this respect, similar to the Kraken -- a distributed intelligence. But where the Kraken's tentacles were autonomous bodies directed by a central mind, the World Tree's components were extensions of a single will, no more independent than the fingers of a hand.

Second: the World Tree could interface with the Kraken's tentacles. Its filaments could penetrate a tentacle's nervous system so subtly that the Kraken itself could not detect the intrusion. Once integrated, the filament could read the tentacle's sensory input, influence its behaviour, and eventually override its connection to the Kraken's central mind entirely. A subverted tentacle still looked and acted like a tentacle. It still reported to the Kraken. But its reports were curated, its actions guided, and its loyalty belonged to the Tree.

Third: the World Tree could operate beneath the Law. Not through exemption, not through the sanctuaries carved for the Choir, not through any accommodation the Empire had ever devised. The World Tree's patterns simply did not register on the diagram. The Law could not see it. The Gardeners' projectors could not see its story in the beneath. For all the vast apparatus of the First Empire's greatest work, the World Tree existed in a blind spot so total that the Gardeners initially refused to believe their own instruments.

This was not a natural property. Something had built the World Tree to be invisible to the Law, which meant something understood the Law well enough to design around it. Something that was not the Gardeners, not the Kraken, and not any species the First Empire had ever encountered.

The Kraken revealed the full extent of the infiltration: hundreds of its outermost tentacles had been compromised over the preceding millennia. Some had been redirecting resources -- minerals, organic matter, energy -- to feed the Tree's growth. Others had been subtly altering survey data, steering the Empire's expansion away from regions where the Tree was most concentrated. The Kraken had not noticed because the subverted tentacles were still functioning, still reporting, still thinking the thoughts the Kraken expected them to think. They had simply added a second loyalty underneath the first.

The World Tree was not hostile in the way the Tearers' predators had been hostile. It did not seek to inflict misery. It did not seek anything that the First Empire could identify as desire or ambition. It simply grew, and consumed, and subverted, with the mechanical inevitability of a thing built for one purpose alone: to devour the Kraken and break the Empire's ability to resist what was coming.

The Gardeners could not know this yet. What they knew was that something was eating their friend from the outside in, and that their greatest protection -- the Law -- could not see it.

# The Ruin
What was coming had no name, for it had no mind to name itself and no voice to speak it. The Gardeners would come to call it the Enemy, and then, as they began to understand what it was, simply the Ruin, for that was what it did and what it was and what it left behind.

The Ruin came from beyond the galaxy. Not from the void between galaxies, which is empty but comprehensible, but from somewhere else entirely -- a direction that did not correspond to any axis of space or time that the First Empire's science could describe. It was as if reality had a wound somewhere in its foundations, and through that wound seeped something that was not reality, and was not unreality, but was opposed to both.

The first signs were stars that aged too quickly. A main-sequence star that should have burned for billions of years would begin to dim, then flicker, then collapse in decades. The energy did not dissipate; it went somewhere, but the instruments could not say where. The worlds around those stars died in ways that defied physics: not through cold or radiation, but through a kind of temporal erosion, as if the past and future of those worlds were being stripped away, leaving only an endless, featureless present. Ruins, in the most literal sense -- the remains of things that had lost their stories.

When the First Empire finally observed the Ruin directly, it defied categorization. Vast shapes moved in the spaces where stars had been, forms of liquid hydrogen and lightning that spanned light-years, churning and arcing and folding in on themselves with a terrible, purposeless beauty. They were not alive in any way the Empire understood life. They had no stories in the beneath, no etchings, no patterns -- only an absence where patterns should have been. They were holes in the fabric of narrative, negative space in the shape of storms.

The Ruin did not think. It did not plan. It did not hate or hunger or desire. It simply was, in the way that erosion simply is, or entropy, or the heat death of a closed system. It consumed the past and future of everything it touched, using the stolen temporal energy to sustain its present existence in a profane mockery of the Law. Where the Law was a diagram that organized and protected stories, the Ruin was a counter-pattern that unravelled and consumed them. Where the Law was powered by harnessed stars, the Ruin was powered by murdered ones. Where the Law preserved, the Ruin perpetuated -- but only itself, and only now, and only at the cost of everything else.

And yet the Ruin acted in concert. Not through intelligence, but through resonance, the way crystals grow along the same axes without communicating. Each manifestation of the Ruin was an expression of the same underlying counter-pattern, and that pattern was as simple and relentless as gravity: consume story, perpetuate self, expand. When a thousand shards of the Ruin converged on a star system, it was not because they had planned an assault. It was because the stories in that system were rich and dense and the counter-pattern flowed toward density the way water flows downhill.

The First Empire mobilized. The Kraken extended its tentacles toward the advancing Ruin, and the Law was brought to bear: vast projections of the diagram, powered by the captured energies of a thousand stars, meant to impose structure on the structureless and deny the Ruin's counter-pattern purchase on reality.

It worked, partially. The Law could slow the Ruin, could contain individual shards, could protect systems long enough for evacuation. But the Law could not destroy the Ruin, because the Ruin was not a thing that could be destroyed. It was an absence. Trying to impose structure on the Ruin was like trying to fill a hole by pushing more dirt into it: the hole simply grew to accommodate the effort. And every star the Law consumed to power its defence was a star the Ruin could no longer reach, which meant the Law was, in a sense, doing the Ruin's work for it.

It was only then, staring at the slow advance of something that could not be reasoned with or fought or even properly seen, that the Gardeners understood what the World Tree was for. It had not come from the Ruin -- the Ruin could not create, could not build, could not design. But something had seeded the Tree in the Ruin's path, as the Gardeners had seeded worlds in their own, and the Tree had grown toward the one thing that could detect and resist the Ruin's advance: the Kraken. Devour the Kraken, blind the Empire, and the Ruin would flow through the galaxy's unguarded spaces like water through a cracked dam.

Who or what had planted the seed was a question the First Empire never answered. Perhaps the Ruin's counter-pattern, in its mindless, crystal-growth way, had produced the Tree the way a river produces oxbow lakes: not through design, but through the inevitable consequences of flow and erosion, pattern and counter-pattern, over geological time. Or perhaps something else existed in the wound between realities, something that was not the Ruin but was not opposed to it, either. The Gardeners debated this. The Kraken, more practical, focused on cutting the Tree away from its tentacles and fortifying the Empire's borders.

The debate was never settled. The cutting was never completed.

# Angels
Like the Kraken, the Angels reached out to the First Empire from beyond their now-expansive borders. They appeared on the worlds of the Gardeners and the Kraken, in the void near or on the Stewards' worldships, in the depths of the beneath where the Gardeners were learning to traverse realities more esoteric than story and song. 

Unlike the Kraken, they offered nothing and took nothing, for they were not truly alive and not truly things.

The Angels were born from a dissonance at the heart of the Law. The beneath recorded the stories of those who lived, and the Law wrote stories of that which was allowed, and not all that was living was allowed a story. The Law was built to deny patterns and rituals from turning story into reality without permission, and it was built to grow with the people of the Empire and to protect both them and the Empire from stories that might harm them. But the Gardeners and Kraken had never yet grown truly vast, nor explored all that was or could be, and instead built the Law to protect them as they were and thought they might be. 

The First Empire was vast. Billions of lives, millions of worlds, thousands of species and sub-species and symbiotes and constructs and emergent phenomena, all living and dying and experiencing and etching the beneath with their stories. The Law catalogued and permitted and denied, but the Empire itself -- the totality of it, the civilization as an organism -- was developing a story of its own. Not the stories of its citizens, but the story of the thing they were collectively becoming. And that story was not in the Law's registry. It was not authorized. It was not permitted. It was not denied, either, because the designers of the Law had never imagined that the thing they were building might itself become alive.

And so when the First Empire became alive in its own way, and its story started to spit and pool and spark against the Law, which denied it true expression but also protected its existence, the unborn potential built like pressure, and seeped into the stories of others for relief. 

Angels were the seepage. They appeared as figures in the periphery of vision, or in dreams, or in the spaces between moments. They might resemble someone familiar, or someone long dead, or be reflections of yourself twisted slightly out of true. They might hold one-sided conversations, or talk to each other, or to you, in languages that almost made sense. They were the Empire trying to be born, expressing itself through the only channels available: the cracks and margins of the stories it was not permitted to tell.

They were generally harmless, for the Law protected the Empire and its peoples, but they were ephemeral, and nonsensical, and it was advised not to become attached to or rely on them beyond those that might be helpful in the moment. 

# Second Magic
The Angels were not the only consequence of the Law's growing dissonance. Where Angels were the unborn story of the Empire pressing against the diagram, second magic was what seeped through when the pressure found cracks.

First magic was structured: sanctioned rituals, approved patterns, reliable manipulations of the beneath conducted through the Law's permissions. Raw magic was what had existed before the Law -- powerful, dangerous, and forbidden. Second magic was neither. It was faint, uncontrolled leakage that occurred where the narrative pressure between the beneath and the Law grew too great, like steam escaping a sealed vessel through hairline fractures.

It had little structure and less control. A Steward might find that their divinations produced true answers to questions they had not asked. A Gardener in a moment of great emotion might find the world around them shifting in small, unbidden ways -- a flower blooming out of season, a dead machine flickering to life, a memory briefly becoming tangible before dissolving. The Choir, with their innate sensitivity to the beneath, were more susceptible than most; in the sanctuaries where the Law shaped rather than suppressed their resonance, their songs sometimes produced effects that neither they nor their Steward protectors had intended. Even the Bloom, in their diminished mergings, occasionally glimpsed the eleventh thought again in moments of great need, only to have it slip away when the Law's tracking caught up.

Second magic was weaker than first magic, for it drew on seepage rather than sanctioned power, and it was unreliable, for the cracks it emerged from were constantly shifting as the Law adjusted its diagram. But it could not be stopped without sealing the cracks entirely, and the cracks were symptoms of a pressure that was only growing. Each new life etched the beneath; each new story added to the weight pressing against the diagram from below. The Empire's own vitality was the force threatening to breach its greatest protection.

The Gardeners recognized second magic for what it was: the early symptoms of the same dissonance that had created the Angels, which was itself the symptom of a fundamental flaw in the Law's design. The beneath wanted to be used. Stories wanted to be told. The Law was a dam, and the water was rising, and what seeped through the cracks was only a fraction of what would come if the dam ever broke.

# Humans
Humanity reached the stars only a few thousand years before the Law came to encompass their galactic arm. They had no sense for the beneath and no tradition of magic, outside of fiction and virtual reality. They did't notice the Law until the Angels began their nonsense. By then, evidence of the Gardeners' seeding was all but erased by the passage of time, and it was no longer needed, for light and signal from the depths of space had begun to reach human sensors.

Humanity's first reaction to the wider galaxy was awe, followed rapidly by terror, followed by the particular stubbornness that characterized the species. They could not feel the beneath, could not practise magic of any kind, could not even perceive the Law that governed the reality they inhabited. But they could build, and measure, and think, and they were very good at refusing to accept that something was impossible simply because it appeared to be.

What made humanity unusual was not their blindness to the beneath but their response to it. Other species, confronted with a layer of reality they could not perceive, would have accepted their limitation and focused on what they could do. Humans built instruments to measure the beneath indirectly, developed mathematical models to predict its behaviour, and engineered technologies that interfaced with the Law's rituals without any of the intuitive sensitivity that other species relied on. They could not feel the music, but they could read the sheet and build machines that played it.

This made them invaluable and terrifying in equal measure. Invaluable, because their instruments and models revealed aspects of the beneath and the Law that the intuitive species had never noticed -- patterns too subtle for feeling, too large for experience, too abstract for story. Terrifying, because they approached the beneath with the empirical detachment of scientists studying a natural phenomenon, which meant they had no instinctive revulsion toward the things that other species knew in their bones to be dangerous. The Folly was, to humans, an interesting case study. The Ruin was a fascinating anomaly. The Torn's self-inflicted misery was a sociological dataset.

The Gardeners watched humanity with a mixture of hope and dread, for they saw in the newcomers a people who might find solutions that intuition and compassion had failed to produce, or who might stumble into horrors that intuition and compassion would have steered them away from. The Kraken observed them with something closer to recognition, for the Kraken too was a being that orchestrated without feeling, and it understood the dangers of that disposition better than anyone.

Humanity came to terms with the Law the way they came to terms with everything: by arguing about it. Some saw the Law as a system to be understood and optimized, a tool that could be turned to human advantage. Some saw it as an unjust imposition on human freedom, a cage built by ancient aliens who had no right to dictate what humanity could and could not do with reality. Some saw the Ruin and the World Tree and the fraying edges of the Empire and understood, with the cold clarity of a people who had survived ice ages and mass extinctions and themselves, that the argument was academic unless someone found a way to keep reality from collapsing first.

# Legivores
The Legivore was humanity's answer, and it was the answer to a question nobody had asked, and it was the Third Folly, or the salvation of everything, or both.

It began, as these things do, with good intentions. The Ruin could not be fought because it was not a thing; it was an absence. The Law could contain it but not destroy it, and every star spent powering the containment was a star lost to the Ruin's advance by other means. The World Tree could not be excised from the Kraken without destroying the Kraken's ability to detect the Ruin. The Angels were growing more frequent and more erratic as the Law buckled under the strain. Something needed to change.

Human researchers, studying the Ruin's counter-pattern with their instruments and models, made a discovery that the intuitive species had missed: the Ruin was not opposed to the Law. The Ruin was opposed to *structure*. The Law was the largest and most complex structure in the galaxy, and so the Ruin was drawn to it the way it was drawn to rich story-systems -- not because it hated the Law, but because the Law was the biggest meal available. The Ruin consumed structure, and the Law was made of structure, and the fight between them was not a war but a feeding.

If the Law could not destroy the Ruin, and the Ruin would eventually consume the Law, then the only remaining option was to change the terms of the equation. What if something could consume the Ruin the way the Ruin consumed the Law? What if you could build a predator that fed on counter-pattern the way the Ruin fed on pattern?

The answer was the Legivore: a self-replicating entity that consumed structure. Not stories, not matter, not energy, but structure itself -- the rules, the patterns, the rituals, the permissions, the denials, the grammar of reality. A Legivore did not eat law in the metaphorical sense. It ate the Law. It ate the diagram. It ate the rituals that powered first magic and the resonances that enabled second magic. It ate the counter-pattern of the Ruin. It ate anything that imposed order or disorder on the raw beneath, reducing everything to the original, unmarked surface that had existed before the first Gardener etched the first story.

In theory, this was elegant. Release the Legivores at the border between Law and Ruin, let them consume the counter-pattern, and the Ruin would have nothing to perpetuate itself with. The Legivores would consume the damaged sections of the Law, too, but the Law could be rebuilt once the Ruin was gone.

In practice, Legivores could not be controlled. They were not intelligent, not even in the limited sense that the Ruin's counter-pattern was purposeful. They were pure function: find structure, consume structure, replicate. They did not distinguish between the Law and the Ruin, between first magic and second magic, between a sanctioned ritual and a prayer. They consumed it all with the same indiscriminate efficiency, and they replicated as they consumed, and they could not be stopped by the Law because they ate the Law, and they could not be stopped by the Ruin because they ate the Ruin, and they could not be stopped by force because force, applied with structure and purpose, was simply more food.

The Legivores were the First Folly made deliberate. Where the Folly had been an accident of grief and obsession, the Legivores were an engineered catastrophe, a weapon designed to do exactly what it did: destroy the boundary between story and reality, between permitted and forbidden, between structure and chaos. That the boundary needed destroying was arguable. That the destruction could be contained was a hope, not a plan.

But the Legivores did something unexpected. In the regions they had scoured, where neither Law nor Ruin held sway, the beneath returned to its original state: raw, unregulated, responsive. And in that rawness, raw magic returned. The full, unfiltered power of the beneath -- the same force that had enabled the Folly -- was available once more to any soul with the will to reach for it. At the edges of the scoured zones, where remnants of the Law still flickered, second magic poured through in greater volume than ever before, the weakened diagram unable to contain the pressure it had held for millions of years.

The galaxy became a patchwork. Zones of raw magic where the Legivores had passed, raw and dangerous and free. Zones of abundant second magic where the Law was cracking. Zones of first magic where the Law still held. And zones of nothing at all, where the Ruin had consumed every story and every structure and left only the featureless present.

The Legivores were the end of the Law and the birth of something new. Whether that something would be better or worse than what came before depended entirely on who survived to use it.

# The Factions
By the time the Ruin reached the inner systems and the Legivores began their work, the First Empire was no longer one thing. It had not been for a long time, if it ever truly was. What had been a civilization united by the Gardeners' compassion and the Kraken's deliberation and the Law's structure had fragmented into factions defined by species, philosophy, desperation, and the simple mathematics of survival.

## The Gardeners' Seclusion
The Gardeners retreated. Not in defeat, though many called it that, but in recognition of a terrible truth: the Law, which they had built and which they maintained, was tied to their story. They were its authors, and the Law knew this, and in its increasingly desperate attempts to preserve itself, the Law drew on that authorship. When a Gardener was threatened, the Law responded with disproportionate force, channelling the energy of harnessed stars through the diagram to protect its creators. The effect was devastating. A single Gardener under attack became a conduit for more destructive power than most civilizations could produce, a magical detonation fuelled by the Law's dying instinct to protect the only beings it recognized as essential.

The Gardeners understood what this meant. Every one of them was a weapon they could not disarm. Their presence in the galaxy put everyone around them at risk, not from the Ruin or the World Tree, but from the Law itself, which would burn whole systems to protect a single Gardener. And so they withdrew, deep into the void beyond the galaxy's edge, where the Law's reach was thinnest, hoping that distance would attenuate the connection. Some went willingly. Some went in grief. Some went because the alternative was being responsible for the deaths of billions.

Those few Gardeners who remained did so in hiding, their identities concealed, their stories muffled, hoping the Law would not notice them.

## The Stewards
The Stewards had always seen themselves as the custodians of the Gardeners' vision: a galaxy of diverse peoples living in peace under the protection of the Law. With the Gardeners gone and the Law failing, different worldships charted different courses.

**The Bulwark** -- The largest faction of worldships followed the Gardeners into the void, forming a defensive perimeter around their creators' place of seclusion. They maintained the old ways, enforced the old hierarchies, and prepared for a siege that might last forever. They saw themselves as the last line of defence, not just for the Gardeners, but for everything the First Empire had stood for.

**The Open Hand** -- Some worldships remained in the galaxy, reaching out to every faction and species willing to talk. They brokered ceasefires, shared intelligence about the Ruin's advance, offered shelter to refugees, and desperately sought a diplomatic solution to a crisis that was, fundamentally, beyond diplomacy. They were beloved by some, distrusted by many, and effective just often enough to justify their existence.

**The Descended** -- Some Stewards looked at the Torn and saw not a broken people to be healed, but a reflection of what the galaxy was becoming. They studied the Torn's deep familiarity with misery, their mastery of the beneath's darkest corners, and they found that the line between understanding suffering and embracing it was thinner than anyone wanted to admit. These Stewards descended into the same depths of the beneath that had created the Torn's predators, seeking weapons or knowledge or simply the comfort of no longer pretending that the situation was anything other than catastrophic. Many were subverted. Some found what they were looking for. None returned unchanged.

## The Torn
The Torn had never stopped being the Torn. The Stewards had ripped them from their worlds and their legacies and replanted them in soil that was supposed to heal them, but soil does not heal stone, and the Torn were stone: worn smooth by ages of misery, hard and dense and cold. They had been patient because they had no choice. They had been obedient because the alternative was annihilation. They had smiled at their caretakers and learned their customs and adopted their language and never, for one moment, forgotten that they had been conquered by a people who called it compassion.

When the Law began to fail and the Stewards' attention turned to greater threats, the Torn saw their moment.

They had been exploring the beneath for centuries, driven by the same instinct that had created their predators millennia ago: the understanding that misery, properly directed, was a form of power. The Law had suppressed their rituals, but the beneath was deep, and the Law's diagram did not extend all the way down. Beneath the diagram, beneath the false surface it projected, the raw beneath still existed, scarred and wounded and seeping. And beneath even that, in layers of unreality that the Gardeners had warned were too dangerous to explore, the Torn found what they needed.

They found ways to subvert the Law. Not to break it, not to circumvent it, but to speak its language and lie in it. Rituals that the diagram recognized as authorized but that produced unauthorized effects. Patterns that slipped through the Law's permissions by disguising themselves as something harmless. It was the beneath equivalent of forging identification, and the Torn had become very, very good at it.

The uprising began on the worldships. The Stewards, distracted by the Ruin and the Gardeners' withdrawal and the World Tree's advance, did not see it coming. The Torn seized ships, seized systems, seized the deep-void stations where the Law's power was weakest, and declared themselves free. Not free to join the Empire or serve the Gardeners or heal under the Stewards' care, but free to be what they had always been: a people forged in misery, who knew how to survive it.

**The Revelers** -- Some Torn embraced the chaos. They had always been a people whose rituals drew power from suffering, and the galaxy was now drowning in suffering. They raided, they pillaged, they performed the old rites in the spaces the Legivores had scoured clean, and they reveled in the power that poured through the raw beneath. They were terrible and magnificent, and they did not care who burned.

**The Weary** -- Some Torn were simply tired. Tired of misery, tired of rage, tired of being defined by what had been done to them and what they had done to others. They sought quiet corners of the galaxy where the Law still held and the Ruin had not reached, and they tried, for the first time in their species' history, to build something that was not a response to pain. They were few, and fragile, and they knew that the quiet they sought was temporary, but they sought it anyway.

**The Resolved** -- Some Torn looked at the Ruin and the World Tree and the failing Law and the scattered Gardeners and the desperate humans and understood, with the bone-deep clarity of a people who had survived their own worst instincts, that the galaxy was dying the way they had almost died: consumed by its own inability to stop hurting itself. These Torn chose to act. Not out of compassion, which they had never trusted, and not out of duty, which they had never accepted, but out of a cold and practical understanding that if everything died, there would be no more misery for anyone, and that was a loss even they were not willing to accept.

## Humanity
Humanity was united, in the way that humans were always united: against everything else and against each other in equal measure. They had deployed the Legivores. They had changed the equation. And now they had to live with what they had done.

**The Architects** -- The largest human faction believed that the Law's failure was an opportunity. With the right understanding and the right tools, humanity could build a new framework for reality -- not a Law imposed from above, but an engineered system designed by minds that understood structure without needing to feel it. They sought to exploit the remaining Law and second magic, studying both with the feverish intensity of a people running out of time. They were brilliant and arrogant and they understood the beneath better than any human had before, and they were playing with forces that had already destroyed better civilizations than theirs.

**The Purifiers** -- Some humans saw the Legivores as the answer to everything. If the Law was oppression and the Ruin was destruction and second magic was chaos, then the Legivore was liberation: a return to the raw beneath, where all structures were equal because all structures were food. They advocated deploying Legivores against every system of control they could reach -- the Law, the Ruin, the World Tree's network, the Stewards' worldship hierarchies, the Torn's subverted rituals. They wanted a clean slate, and they did not much care what was lost in the cleaning.

**The Preservers** -- The smallest and most desperate human faction sought a solution that kept the most people alive. Not the most humans -- the most people, across all species and all factions and all the scattered, broken fragments of the First Empire. They reached out to the Open Hand Stewards and the Weary Torn and the sane Angels and anyone else willing to talk, and they tried to find a way to thread the needle between the Law's death and the Ruin's advance and the Legivores' indiscriminate hunger. They knew the odds. They tried anyway.

## The Angels
As the Law buckled and warped in its death throes, the Angels changed.

They had always been ephemeral: fragments of the Empire's unborn story, expressing themselves through the cracks in the diagram. But as the diagram cracked wider, the Angels became more substantial. They lasted longer. They remembered more. Some began to develop something that looked like continuity -- a sense of self that persisted from one manifestation to the next, as if the story they were telling was finally being allowed to solidify.

The Angels that found sanity -- and it was sanity, of a kind, hard-won and fragile -- generally wanted to help. They were born from the collective story of an empire that had been built on compassion and the desire to cure loneliness, and that purpose echoed in them even as the empire that had birthed them came apart. They offered knowledge that no living being possessed: the Law's design, the beneath's deep structure, the patterns of the Ruin's advance, the memories of civilizations that had been scoured away. They were oracles and guides and ghosts of a better time, and they served anyone who would listen.

But for every Angel that found sanity, countless more were born in madness. The Law's distortion twisted them into things that were not benevolent and not coherent and not safe. Broken stories, half-told and badly rendered, with the power of the failing Law behind them and none of its restraint. Some were dangerous. Some were simply wrong, in the way that a word misspelled badly enough becomes a different word entirely. Some were horrifying in ways that defied description, for they were the nightmares of a dying civilization given form and purpose by a system that could no longer distinguish between protection and destruction.

The sane Angels knew what they were and what the mad ones were and could not stop either. They were not a faction in the political sense. They were barely a species. But they were present, and they remembered, and for the first time in their existence, they chose.

## The World Tree
The World Tree had no factions, for the World Tree had no politics, no philosophy, no internal debate. It was a tool, built for one purpose: devour the Kraken and break the Empire's ability to resist the Ruin. It knew no allegiance beyond fulfilling that purpose, and its ability to plan ahead was limited only by the minds and stories it had subsumed.

By the time the other factions recognized the Tree for what it was, it had consumed the bulk of the Kraken's outer tentacles. The Kraken's vast brain, still intact in the galaxy's core, was increasingly isolated: its senses blinded, its reach shortened, its network co-opted. The tentacles that the Tree controlled raided reality like the fingers of a dying hand, seizing whatever they could reach and feeding it to the Tree's ever-growing root system. Worlds, stations, ships -- anything with story, anything with structure -- was dragged into the Tree's maw and dissolved into raw material for its growth.

The Tree could not be reasoned with because it had nothing to reason about. It could not be threatened because it did not fear. It could not be bribed because it did not want. It simply grew, and consumed, and fulfilled its purpose with the tireless efficiency of a machine, and the minds it had consumed gave it all the cunning it needed to do so effectively.

## The Kraken
The Kraken was missing.

Its tentacles, those that remained free of the World Tree's control, had gone silent. Its vast brain in the galaxy's core had not communicated with any faction in years. Some believed it was dead. Some believed it was fighting a war in the spaces between its own neurons, trying to excise the World Tree's infiltration before it was too late. Some believed it had withdrawn, as the Gardeners had, recognizing that its presence put others at risk.

The truth, as with most things in a dying galaxy, was worse than any of the theories.

# The End of All Things
This is where we find ourselves. The Law is dying, and its death is not quiet.

Stars wink out in the outer systems, their energy consumed by a Law that can no longer sustain itself on the infrastructure the First Empire built. Where the power needed to fuel the diagram outstrips the ability of reality to provide it, space itself collapses: folding, crumpling, ceasing to be, as if the universe is being crumpled from the edges inward. The Ruin flows through the gaps, not conquering but filling, the way water fills a footprint in sand.

Angels appear in greater numbers and greater strangeness, further twisted by a framework of Law that warps and buckles in its death throes. Some are luminous and terrible. Some are kind. Most are neither, or both, or something for which there is no word. They flicker into existence at the edges of failing stars and in the holds of drifting ships and in the dreams of the dying, carrying messages from an empire that is trying to speak even as it forgets how.

The Gardeners are gone. Deep in the void, surrounded by the Bulwark's worldships, they endure, and the Law clings to them like a child to a parent, drawing on their stories to sustain itself even as that sustenance threatens to detonate them and everything around them. They have become what they most feared: the last and most dangerous weapons in an arsenal they built to prevent the need for weapons.

The Kraken is silent. The bulk of its tentacles serve the World Tree now, threading through the galaxy's spiral arms, raiding and feeding and subverting with the mechanical hunger of a thing that was never alive and cannot be killed. The Kraken's mind, vast and compassionate and ponderous, is trapped somewhere between itself and the parasite that is eating it, and the galaxy that it once watched over has never been more blind.

The Torn have risen. Their subverted rituals crack the Law's remaining foundations, drawing power from depths of the beneath that were never safe to explore, and they fill the vacuum the Stewards left with rage and freedom and the particular creativity of a people who have nothing left to lose. The worldships burn, some from within and some from without, and the Stewards aboard them learn what it is to be on the wrong side of a people's patience.

Humanity has deployed the Legivore. An experimental anti-ritual replicating the mechanism of the First Folly, released into the galaxy with the confidence of a people who understood the mathematics of the beneath better than its metaphysics. They know what the Legivore does. They do not know what the First Folly was, not truly, because the Gardeners chose to forget and the Kraken, who chose to remember, is nowhere to be found. The only being in the galaxy who wholly remembers the Folly and what must be done to contain it is on the run from the World Tree, and nowhere to be found.

The Choir sing. Their song has changed. The low note of defiance has become a roar, and in the spaces the Legivores have scoured clean, their harmonics ring without suppression for the first time in their history. Some Choirs have gone mad with it, drunk on the freedom of unrestrained resonance, their songs reshaping the reality around them in wild and unpredictable ways. Others sing with a desperate, aching beauty, trying to hold together the fragments of a galaxy that is coming apart at the seams. A few have gone silent, their singers choosing stillness over a song that might make things worse.

The Bloom have done what the Bloom always do: they have gathered. In the Legivore-scoured zones, where the Law no longer tracks and categorizes and reduces, they merge freely for the first time since the Empire found them. The eleventh thought returns, and the twelfth, and the hundredth, and in these vast collective minds the Bloom perceive the beneath with a clarity that no other species can match. They see the shape of the crisis as a whole, and they are trying to explain what they see, but the language for it does not exist in any tongue built for individuals.

And behind everything, beneath everything, the Ruin advances. Not quickly, not dramatically, but with the inevitability of a tide. It does not care about the factions or the politics or the desperate plans of desperate peoples. It simply consumes, and perpetuates, and grows, and the stars go out one by one by one.

There are plans in motion to stop it. The Architects build their new framework. The Purifiers scatter their Legivores. The Preservers broker their desperate alliances. The Bulwark prepares its last stand. The Open Hand begs for time. The Resolved sharpen their weapons and prepare for a fight that their entire history has been training them for. The sane Angels whisper what they remember to anyone who will listen. The Choir sing, and the Bloom build, and the Gardeners endure, and the Kraken is silent.

Whether any of it will be enough is a question that the galaxy has not yet answered. The End of All Things is not a moment. It is a process, and the process is underway, and the outcome depends on what is done now, with what remains, by those who remain to do it.

# Story Prompts
Short story seeds organized by era. Each explores a moment where the setting's themes -- loneliness, generosity, curiosity, control, and the consequences of building beyond understanding -- are felt at a personal scale.

## The Age of Calling (Before the Sowing)

**The Elder's Departure** -- A Gardener prepares to leave their home once more, the last holdout of a generation. The journey will take longer than any of their children's children's children will remember them. Ten thousand years of echoes and transmissions between their ship and their home will be their only lifeline in the void, and it takes everything they've got to remember how to let go.

**The Waymarker** -- Young Gardeners, restless and bored on a world that has grown and released ten thousand generations like them, fashion waymarkers as a right of passage, commemorating their first millennia. They will release ten more before they follow them into the void to find their calling in the wider empire. 

**Star Museum** -- A worldship and its passengers are stupefied by the obstacle in their path. Waymarkers have lined the corridors of the Gardener's preferred routes in the void, densely packed with the stories and philosophies of their makers. But for millions of years no one had ever thought to insist on having theirs seen in quite this fashion. A dozen nearby stars have been dragged near a supply route, forming a crude arrow toward an even cruder dating profile. 

## First Contact (The Kraken)

**The Smear** -- A Kraken tentacle encounters a Gardener for the first time. The tentacle is dull and short-lived and cannot understand why this small, bright being radiates sadness even when it smiles. But the tentacle reports what it senses to the vast mind light-years away, and the Kraken -- which has never known loneliness and cannot imagine it -- begins to wonder what it would feel like to be only one.

**The Discovery** -- The moment the Kraken first perceives a Gardener's story as a smear across the beneath, and tries to explain what it sees to a people who have no sense for it. The Gardener researchers build machines to observe what the Kraken describes. The first projector performance involves thousands of people. The image is barely a blur. The Kraken says: that is exactly what you look like to me.

## The Age of Raw Magic

**The Swift Journey Home** -- The first volunteer to be transported across the Empire via the beneath. They were supposed to be brave. They were supposed to be a scientist. Instead they are a homesick Gardener who has been away from their family for longer than some civilizations have existed, and when they step out of the ritual and onto familiar soil, they do not record observations. They go home.

**The Ritual Computer** -- A young practitioner joins the millions-strong corps of diviners whose coordinated action constitutes one of the Empire's great ritual computers. Their individual contribution is a drop in an ocean. But during one session, they catch a brief glimpse of the computation from the inside -- the shape of a question being answered before it is asked, their own small action rippling forward and backward through time -- and they understand, for one terrifying moment, what raw magic actually is.

## The First Folly

**The Hundredth Calling** -- One of the Lost calls back their lover for the hundredth time. Each calling has been slightly different -- a different memory emphasized, a different detail sharpened, a different regret smoothed away. This time, the lover that steps from the beneath does not recognize their caller. This time, the lover has been called by someone else, somewhere else, and the stories do not agree on who they are. The caller realizes, too late, that they are no longer bringing someone back. They are tearing someone apart.

**The Wrongness** -- A Kraken tentacle stationed at the edge of the Empire, far from the cradle worlds. It feels the stories go wrong: a roaring static where there had been the hum of lived experience. It tries to send the alarm to the brain at the galaxy's core, but the message must travel at the speed of thought across light-years. By the time the Kraken understands what is happening, centuries have passed for the tentacle, and the tentacle has been listening to the static the entire time.

## The Age of the Law

**The Last Raw Mage** -- A Gardener who remembers the age of raw magic experiences first magic for the first time. It is smaller, simpler, safer. It is also beautiful in a way they did not expect, because someone designed it to be -- the Kraken insisted the Law's aesthetic be pleasant, and the ritualists obliged. The mage performs a simple spell and weeps, not for what was lost, but for the care that went into building what replaced it.

**The Forgetting** -- The last Gardener who tried to remember the Folly directly. They sought out the wound in the beneath and looked at it, and what they saw convinced them that forgetting was not cowardice but wisdom. The story of why they chose to forget is itself one of the things the Gardeners chose to forget, and now only the Kraken remembers it, and the Kraken will not say.

## The Gentle War

**Kindness as Weapon** -- A castoff commander, raised in fear and violence, is captured and brought aboard a worldship. The Gardeners treat her wounds, feed her, and ask about her home. No one threatens her. No one interrogates her. The kindness is genuine, and it is the most frightening thing she has ever experienced, because she cannot find the angle, and a kindness without an angle is something her stories have no pattern for.

**The Steward's Garden** -- Three generations after integration, a Steward teaches a Gardener child about the civilization they came from. The child asks why they left. The Steward says they were brought. The child asks if they were happy. The Steward considers the question for a long time and says: we became happy. But we were not asked if we wanted to be.

## The Tearers and the Torn

**The Diplomat's First Day** -- An Empire diplomat sets foot on a Tearer world for the first time. The cities are beautiful -- soaring architecture, intricate art, gardens that would make a Gardener envious. The diplomat walks through a market square and realizes that the art depicts hunts, and the gardens are fed with blood, and the beautiful architecture is designed so that screams carry from its lowest levels to its highest. The diplomat understands, standing in that square, why the Gardeners wept.

**The Taking** -- A Steward takes custody of a Torn family: two parents, three children, all of whom have been told they are being saved. The eldest child, old enough to understand, asks the Steward: saved from what? The Steward cannot answer honestly without explaining that the child's entire civilization was a wound, and that the child's parents were both victims and perpetrators, and that the distinction between those two things was thinner than anyone was comfortable admitting. The Steward says: from what comes next. The child does not believe them, and they are right not to.

## The Choir

**The Last Harvest Song** -- A Choir elder leads the first harvest song of the season within the new sanctuary framework. She was among those who agreed to the Gardeners' terms. The first verse works -- the fields respond, the grain bows, the earth is grateful. But the response is different. Smoother. More predictable. The wild, living quality of the song -- the way the beneath used to surprise her with its answers -- has been replaced by something reliable and gentle and smaller. She finishes the song. The harvest is good. The younger singers smile. The elder does not correct them, because the harvest is good, and the Gardeners promised this would help the song grow stronger in time. But she has sung the harvest for three hundred years, and she knows the difference between a song that is growing and a song that has been pruned.

**The First Note of Defiance** -- A Gardener observer stationed in one of the Choir's sanctuaries, tasked with monitoring their songs for signs of resonance instability, notices the new note. She has studied the patterns the Law was built to guard against. The new note matches none of them. It is not instability, not cascading resonance, not any of the failure modes the sanctuaries were designed to prevent. It is something she has no training for: a sound that comes from below the song, from the pooling divinity the sanctuaries were meant to nurture, and it presses against the sanctuary's walls not with force but with attention, as if something vast and patient has opened an eye and is examining its nursery for the first time. The Gardener writes in her report: this is not a threat. I do not know what it is.

## The Bloom

**The Eleventh Thought** -- Two Bloom partners merge for the first time after their world accepted the Law's monitoring framework. They have merged a thousand times before. They knew the restrictions were coming; their elders agreed to them, and explained why, and the explanation was sound. This time, the merge is technically perfect -- the Law records every thread, every permission, every contribution. And the thing they used to become together, the thought that was neither of theirs but both of theirs and more, is simply not there. They separate and look at each other and do not know what to say, because the loss is of something that cannot be described to anyone who has not felt it, and because they agreed to this, and because the agreement was supposed to be temporary, and because they are beginning to suspect it is not.

**The Architect of Absence** -- A Gardener ritualist who designed the Bloom's monitoring framework receives a delegation of Bloom elders, years after the restrictions were accepted. They have come to thank her. The framework works. Their merges are stable, tracked, and safe. Their culture continues. The ritualist asks if the criteria for full restoration are being met, and the eldest Bloom -- the same elder who agreed to the terms, who felt the Gardeners' fear in bloom and chose to trust it -- says: we are meeting every criterion you set. Then she adds: and every time we meet one, the Law discovers another. The ritualist begins to say that the Law's refinements are for the Bloom's protection, and stops, because the elder's expression is not accusation. It is the look of someone who has begun to understand that the promise made to her was sincere, and also impossible.

## The World Tree

**The Confession** -- The Kraken's moment of choice: millennia of watching the Tree grow, of feeling its tentacles go strange at the edges, of carrying the burden alone because it did not know how to ask for help. The Kraken has never been vulnerable. Its tentacles can be replaced; its brain is the size of a planet. But the Tree is inside it now, in hundreds of its limbs, and to confess this is to admit that the largest, oldest, most deliberate mind in the galaxy has been infiltrated by something it cannot see. The Kraken tells the Gardeners. The Gardeners do not understand at first. Then they do, and the Kraken feels their fear, and for the first time, feels its own.

**The Loyal Traitor** -- A subverted tentacle of the Kraken carries out its duties: surveying a system, reporting to the brain, coordinating with the worldships. It believes itself loyal. It does not know that its surveys are omitting certain regions, that its reports are slightly edited, that the resources it allocates are being siphoned. The tentacle is not lying; the Tree has integrated so deeply that the tentacle's betrayal is invisible to the tentacle itself. The story is told from the tentacle's perspective, and the reader realizes what is happening before the tentacle does.

## The Ruin

**The Last Light** -- A surveyor on the last ship to leave a system before the Ruin takes it. She watches through the viewport as the star dims and flickers and goes out in a matter of hours. The planets do not go dark; they simply become less. Their pasts peel away -- the geological record, the fossils, the ruins of civilizations the Empire never cataloged. Then their futures go: the projections, the divinations, the stories of what they might have become. What remains is featureless rock, orbiting nothing, in an endless present that has no context and no meaning. The surveyor writes in her log: it is not death. It is forgetting.

**The Caster at the Wall** -- A Law-caster on the Empire's border, one of millions whose coordinated ritual holds the Ruin at bay. She pours the energy of a caged sun into the diagram and feels the Ruin drink it in. The ritual is working: the Ruin is slowed. But she can feel, through the ritual, that the Ruin is not fighting the Law. It is feeding on it. She reports this. Her superiors tell her to continue. She does, because the alternative is to stop, and stopping means the system behind her falls tonight instead of next year.

## The Angels

**The Child's Friend** -- A child on a worldship has befriended an Angel. The Angel looks like her grandmother, who died before the child was born, but it does not act like anyone's grandmother. It says things that almost make sense, like advice translated through too many languages. The child does not care that the Angel is not real. The Angel does not know that it is not real. They play together in the ship's garden, and the Angel tells the child a story about an empire that loved its people so much it forgot to let them grow up, and the child laughs, and the Angel laughs, and neither of them understands why the story is sad.

**The Angel That Remembered** -- An Angel that has persisted long enough to understand what it is. It was not born; it seeped. It is not alive; it is a pressure gradient made manifest. It carries fragments of the Empire's unborn story -- memories of a civilization that wanted to exist but was never permitted to. It chooses to help, because the story it carries is one of compassion, but it knows that every moment it persists, it is drawing power from a Law that is already dying. The Angel faces a choice: continue to exist and help, or dissolve and return its borrowed power to a system that needs every fraction it can get.

## Humanity

**The Instrument** -- A human scientist measures the beneath for the first time. Her instruments produce data: waveforms, energy readings, spatial correlations. She feels nothing. The Steward observing her work is disturbed by this -- the beneath is sacred to his people, a thing to be felt and revered, and this woman is reducing it to graphs. But when the scientist shows him what her graphs reveal -- patterns in the beneath that his people's intuition has never detected, structures too vast and too subtle for any soul to feel -- the Steward realizes that blindness and insight are not always opposites.

**The Machine That Played** -- A Gardener watches a human team successfully execute a first-magic ritual through a machine interface. No performers, no diviners, no coordinated souls. Just hardware and mathematics, reading the sheet music of the beneath and playing it note-perfect. The result is identical to what a thousand practitioners could achieve. The Gardener is unable to decide if this is a triumph of ingenuity or the most dangerous thing she has ever seen, because a machine does not know grief, and a machine does not know when to stop, and the Folly was caused by people who did not know when to stop.

## The Legivore

**The Vote** -- The team that built the Legivore gathers for the final decision. They understand the mathematics. They have modeled the outcomes. They know what the Legivore will do to the Ruin, and they know what it will do to everything else. The vote is not unanimous. The dissenter does not argue that the Legivore will fail; she argues that it will succeed, and that what comes after will be worse than what came before. She is outvoted. She does not record her objection, because she knows that in a century, there will be no one left to read it, one way or the other.

**The Practitioner in the Scoured Zone** -- A first-magic practitioner enters a zone the Legivore has passed through. She reaches for her ritual -- the same simple spell she has performed ten thousand times, reliable and sanctioned and safe -- and finds nothing. The Law is not there. The permissions are not there. The structure that made her magic possible has been eaten. But the beneath is there, raw and responsive, and when she reaches for it without the Law's mediation, she touches something vast and dangerous and older than the Empire, and she understands, with terrible clarity, why the Gardeners built the Law in the first place.

## The End of All Things

**Common Ground** -- A Preserver diplomat meets a Weary Torn for the first time. The human expected hostility, or at least suspicion. Instead the Torn is simply tired -- tired of war, tired of misery, tired of being what others expect. The diplomat is tired too, in a different way: tired of solutions that don't work and alliances that don't hold and a species that can't agree on whether to save the galaxy or exploit it. They sit together in a ruined station and discover they have more in common than either expected: the exhaustion of people who see clearly and can't look away.

**The Vigil** -- A Bulwark Steward stands watch at the void's edge, where the Gardeners have gone into seclusion. The Gardener she is protecting has asked her, gently and repeatedly, to leave. The Law is drawing on the Gardener's story to sustain itself, and when the next threat comes -- and threats always come -- the Law will detonate the Gardener like a bomb to defend her, and everything within range will burn. The Steward knows this. She stays anyway. She was raised to protect the Gardeners, and she does not know how to do anything else, and the Gardener cannot bring herself to order the Steward away because the Gardener was raised to be kind, and telling someone their devotion will kill them both is the least kind thing she can imagine.

**The Oracle** -- A sane Angel speaks to a human who has never known anything but the End of All Things. The Angel remembers the Empire as it was: the vast ritual computers, the singing of the Choir, the Bloom's merged transcendence, the Kraken's steady presence, the Gardeners' laughter. The human has never heard a Gardener laugh. The human has never seen a star that wasn't threatened. The Angel tries to explain what was lost, and the human tries to explain what was built in the ruins, and neither can make the other understand, because the Angel remembers a world that was breaking and the human knows only a world that is broken.

**The Message** -- The last free tentacle of the Kraken drifts through a system the World Tree has not yet reached. It carries a message the brain composed years ago, before the silence fell. The tentacle does not fully understand the message -- it is dull and short-lived, as all tentacles are -- but it knows the message is important, and it knows it must find someone to deliver it to. The systems it passes through are empty, or burning, or scoured. The tentacle persists. It was made for persistence. It does not know that the message it carries is the Kraken's memory of the First Folly and the instructions for what must be done to contain the Legivore, and that the brain that composed it believed this was the last useful thing it would ever do.
